Assignment 4 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Jack Keeton — jkeeton@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Rich countries have separated economic growth from carbon emissions.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Four hand-picked exemplars, indexed to 1990. Panel A shows only the UK, Sweden, Denmark and Germany, the textbook decoupling success stories, rather than a random or complete sample. Indexing both series to 1990 = 100 puts dollars and tonnes on one axis so the "scissors" shape is readable at a glance, which is a legitimate technique. But 1990 is also a flattering baseline: it falls just before East German heavy industry collapsed after reunification and just before the UK's switch from coal to gas, so part of the drop has little to do with policy-driven decoupling. I considered a full small-multiples grid of every rich country, but the mixed picture blunted the message, so I kept the four strongest cases and let Panel B answer the "cherry-picking" objection.
  • Truncated y-axis, shaded gap, and direct end-labels. The y-axis runs from 30 to 160 rather than from 0, which stretches the vertical distance between the lines so the fall in emissions dominates each panel. Truncation is common for indexed series, so it doesn't read as a trick, and the dashed line at 100 keeps the baseline honest. The green fill between the two lines turns the gap into a visible "dividend," and the end-labels ("GDP/person +X%", "CO2/person −Y%") hand the reader the takeaway without making them read the axis. The labels are accurate; the axis range is the persuasive lever.
  • "It's not just four countries": a second panel with a hand-chosen start year. Panel B pre-empts the obvious objection to Panel A by showing all high-income countries at once, which looks like the opposite of cherry-picking. However, the comparison window starts in 2005, chosen because most rich-country emission declines begin around then; starting in 1990 (to match Panel A) would put more countries above zero. So the panel defends against one kind of selection while quietly relying on another. The title ("It's Not Just Four Countries…") and the warm, calm cream-and-green palette, together with the headline "Prosperity Without Pollution," frame the whole visualization as good news rather than as an open question.
  • Counting every emission decline as a win. Every country whose CO2 per person fell is coloured green, and the title's count ("N of M Rich Nations Now Emit Less Per Person") includes all of them, including countries whose GDP per person also fell over 2005–2018. Those are cases of recession, not decoupling. The title's wording is literally true, but sitting under a decoupling headline it invites the reader to read the count as "N countries decoupled." The shaded "Richer and Cleaner" zone covers only the lower-right quadrant, so a careful reader can spot green dots outside it, but at a glance the green mass reads as one success story. Similarly, the x-axis is capped at +100%; the one country beyond the cap is disclosed in the footnote rather than plotted, which tidies the layout but removes a point from view.
  • Territorial, per-person CO2 as the emissions measure. Both panels use territorial emissions (emissions produced inside a country's borders) per person. This is the standard World Bank measure, and the footnote names it. But it ignores emissions embodied in imports. Rich countries have moved manufacturing to places like China, so consumption-based emissions fall much less than territorial ones. Using per-person figures also hides population growth (the UK and Sweden both grew). Disclosing "territorial" in small grey type is earnest on paper, but few readers know what it implies, so the effect is closer to omission.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Answering a time question with a snapshot. Decoupling is a claim about change over time within countries, yet Panel A is a single-year, cross-country comparison. It shows, accurately, that richer countries emit more per person in 2018, but that says nothing about whether any individual country is bending its curve. The title "The Richer We Get, the More We Burn" completes the swap: "get richer" implies a process over time that the chart does not show. This was the most effective choice on the AGAINST side, because the correlation is strong and visually obvious, and the title never states anything a reader could check against the chart and find false.
  • Log–log axes, a fitted trend line, and honest labels. Income and emissions both span several orders of magnitude, so log scales are the right encoding, and both axes say "log scale." The least-squares line, fit on log values, summarises the relationship transparently. I labelled the United States, China, India and Qatar for context, but also Sweden, a rich country with relatively low emissions. Including a counterexample makes the chart feel balanced, which in turn makes it more persuasive; one labelled exception reads as "the exception that proves the rule."
  • Alarm palette, an arbitrary $30k cut-off, and bubble size without a legend. The dark background with red highlights signals danger before the reader reads a word. "Rich" is defined by a $30,000 GDP-per-person threshold that I chose because it neatly separates a red cluster in the upper right; the World Bank definition is used on the other panel, so the two sides of the visualization quietly use different definitions of "rich." Bubble area is scaled to total emissions with a square-root transform, so areas are proportional, but there is no size legend, so the large red discs (the US above all) register as "a lot" without being readable as a quantity.
  • Totals instead of per person, and 1960 as the comparison point. Panel B switches to total emissions, which grow with population, while the FOR side's per-person figures do not. The title ("Rich Countries Still Burn Twice the Carbon They Did in 1960") compares against 1960 rather than against the late-2000s peak, which is the comparison decoupling advocates actually make. The annotations mark 1960 and 1990 but not the peak, so the decline after it is never named. In addition, the group is defined by today's income classification, so it includes countries that were not rich in 1960 and whose emissions grew as they industrialised. That arguably supports the proposition's critics honestly, but it also inflates the historical growth.
  • A zero-based axis and a complete-series filter, used for persuasive effect. Starting the y-axis at zero is the textbook-earnest choice, and I restricted the sum to countries with a complete 1960–2018 record (count disclosed in the footnote) so countries entering the data partway through do not create fake jumps. Both choices are defensible and stated. But I chose them knowing their effect: with the axis at zero and headroom set to 1.25× the maximum, the post-2007 decline in totals looks like a small wobble rather than a trend. This is the clearest case where a conventionally "honest" technique did persuasive work, which is why I scored it only mildly positive.

Final Reflection

What surprised me most was how little I needed to distort the data. Every number in all four charts is accurately computed from the World Bank data, and none of the axes are mislabelled. The persuasion came almost entirely from choices made before anything was drawn: which countries to include, which start year to use, whether to measure emissions per person or in total, territorially or by consumption, and whether to ask "how do rich and poor countries compare?" or "how have rich countries changed?". The FOR side was easier to build because a real and well-documented trend exists for a subset of countries, and my job was mainly to frame that subset as the whole story. The AGAINST side was harder; the most honest version of the argument depends on consumption-based emissions, which this dataset does not contain. So I had to change the question, using a cross-sectional snapshot and total emissions, and that is where my most deceptive choices ended up.

The second surprise was that earnest techniques did a lot of the persuasive work. Panel B on the FOR side looks like a response to the cherry-picking critique because it shows every rich country, and Panel B on the AGAINST side uses a zero-based axis precisely because it makes a real decline look negligible. Clear footnotes ("territorial emissions," "high-income countries with complete data") made both sides feel more trustworthy while disclosing things most readers are not equipped to interpret. Disclosure that the audience cannot use is technically transparent but does not stop them from being misled, so I no longer think "I put it in the footnote" settles the ethical question.

I now think of ethical visualization less as a property of individual techniques and more as a relationship between the chart and the question it claims to answer. A truncated axis, a chosen start year, or a per-person measure can each be the right choice; they become misleading when they are chosen because of the answer they produce rather than because they fit the question, and when a reasonable reader would reach a different conclusion if the alternative were shown. The bound I would draw is therefore counterfactual: a persuasive choice is acceptable if the designer would be comfortable showing the reader the obvious alternative (the 1990 start year, the full country list, the consumption-based series) next to it, and misleading if the argument only survives as long as the alternative stays hidden. By that test, several of my choices on both sides fail, and I tried to score them accordingly.
